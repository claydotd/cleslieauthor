## Kuang, Carver and Masters


I thought it was worth delving into the books I’ve read this year and attempting to review them somewhat. These are really just personal notes and are by no means meant to be read as an official review. 

I read four books in January and enjoyed all of them to varying degrees. I will start with the most recently published one, as for some reason in the darker months I always want to read more classic novels and feel pretentious and cosy about them. 

### The Main Event

The biggest and most complex book I read in January was by far _Babel_ by RF Kuang, and I was excited to get a chance to borrow it from my local library (hello queue of 41 other readers!). I’ve read all of her books so far, and have sort of been made semi-aware of the internet’s attempt to cancel her over the recent months, but I don’t know the details of it so will leave it there. 

Regarding _Babel_, I did go into this wanting to enjoy it - I loved _Katabasis_ and felt ready to read another somewhat challenging book. I would say that’s what I enjoy about Kuang’s work, both _Katabasis_ and _Babel_ were challenging but enjoyable reads. They reminded me of being in university again (not least because I studied Philosophy, so am definitely predispitioned to either loving or loading _Katabasis_). 

And perhaps that was my downfall - maybe if I’d studied linguistics I would have found _Babel_ more enjoyable because I would have been more immersed in the field. I did find the overall theme really interesting, and really wanted to enjoy the book. The way that Kuang writes is certainly lyrical and beautiful, but I did struggle to get through parts of it. 

Admittedly, and this is entirely petty on my part so forgive me, but there was a section in the novel near the beginning where the main character has the formation of the UK explained to him. We get a detailed description of Wales, England, and Ireland, but nothing but a nod towards Scotland, even though the housekeeper is Scots. This just rubbed me the wrong way - if you’re going to go to the bother of explaining the country why miss out one in particular? This is written largely in jest and is very much a personal vendetta I have with the book and not serious, but still, unfortunately it tainted the first part for me. 

Overall, as with all of Kuang’s books, I felt drawn into this world of Dark Academia undertones and gothic closeness. Her prose was beautiful, and while I didn’t find the story as compelling as I’d hoped, I think that was likely because of my lack of understanding of linguistics. I enjoyed reading a book that was somewhat challenging, and felt a nice flash of nostalgia to being in university (complete with the urge to take notes on the novel!). I probably won’t ever read it again, but wouldn’t say I didn’t enjoy it at least somewhat. 

### The Supporting Acts

I was gifted a copy of _Far, Far from the Mountain Peak_ by John Masters for Christmas, and what an absolute joy of a book to read. It is very much of it’s time, and full of ridiculous melodrama and excitement, but I sped through it regardless. There’s something wonderful about old books like that where a spot of brandy will solve any problem, and folk think it’s appropriate to bop up a mountain in a three piece suit. I would recommend this if you want a simple, easy to read classic that leans towards the high society intrigue of an Austen novel but everyone’s wearing flat caps rather than fancy dresses (I imagine). 

I reread _A Little Princess_ by Frances Hodgson Burnett as my throwback read in January - this was my favourite book when I was a child and I found my original copy when I was at home over the Christmas period. The characterisation and soft pacing make the book feel warm, even though it’s tackling quite serious issues surrounding class. I read that Burnett had had a similar experience growing up, as her father had died suddenly leaving her family to fall into poverty - I think you can feel the personal experience coming through in the novel. It’s a beautiful and sad read, but one that I can’t not come back to time and time again. 

Finally, I read a short story collection by Raymond Carver, _Elephant and Other Stories_ which I sped through somewhat. I hadn’t read anything by Carver before, and like to think about how the structure of short stories work in comparison to longer prose. _Elephant_ stuck out to me most as the one I remember - it sort of reminds me of some of the more absurdist novellas I’ve read in a way, like Nabokov’s _Despair_ or _The Stranger_ by Camus.  

Overall, January was a good month for a mix of vibes in reading, some new and some old, and lots to think about for the next month!
